# Citational Practices and Construction of Authority

### Project Abstract

The present social and rhetorical functions of citations are evident when examining contemporary citational practices, especially the use of bibliometrics to evaluate academic publications. Studying historical citational practices reveals their fundamental and long-standing social and rhetorical functions. This project builds on previous work in documentation theory and the history of notation to explore the citational practices present in a selection of influential pamphlets published in the British Atlantic in the eighteenth century.

In the 1760s and 1770s, the British Atlantic world engaged in a vigorous debate over the political future of the British colonies in North America. The debate over the American question was largely carried out by pamphleteers whose ideas were quickly printed and disseminated. These pamphlets were important cultural documents that contributed to the creation of a nation independent of the British Empire. They connected people and their ideas while simultaneously creating a web of interconnected documents.

Many of the revolutionary pamphlets were written in response to other pamphlets. They also reference other well-known texts for rhetorical purposes. Though written before the formalization and standardization of modern citation practices, the authors employed ad hoc citational practices that allowed readers to trace the genealogy of their thought and borrow arguments from renowned thinkers. Such practices include the use of italics, capital letters, footnotes, and narrative references.

Through close content analysis of a corpus of pamphlets, including works by Paine, Jefferson, and their respondents, citational markers are identified and categorized to reveal how documentary practices served social and political functions, constructing authority and legitimizing political arguments during a period of intense ideological contestation.

Citation relationships identified through this coding are visualized as a network, illustrating patterns of connection across the corpus. This exploratory analysis of documentation provides insight into the evolution of citational practices and how they constructed political and intellectual authority even before citation had a standardized format.
